The Margin Is Part of the Page

On white space, on the notes readers leave in it, and on the case for leaving room.

Every page is two things at once: the text, and the room around the text. Only the first is ever quoted. Nobody cites a margin, and no one has finished a book and praised its gutters. Yet the blank border is the first thing the eye measures, and it settles, before a word is read, whether the page looks like an invitation or a wall.

It is easy to treat that border as whatever is left over once the words have been poured in. Printers have always known better. A margin is drawn first, and the text is set inside it, in the same way that a room is built before it is furnished. The empty part of the page is not where the design stops. It is where the design starts.

01The empty part does work

A margin has ordinary jobs. It gives the thumbs somewhere to hold the book without covering the words. It keeps the text clear of the edge, where paper is trimmed, worn and stained. It lets the eye find the start of the next line, because a line that begins at a calm, straight edge is easier to return to than one that begins in clutter.

It also sets the length of the line, which matters more than almost anything else on the page. A line that is too long loses the reader on the journey back to the left edge; one that is too short breaks every thought into pieces. The usual advice is a line of somewhere between forty-five and seventy-five characters, and the simplest way to keep to it on a wide sheet is not to fill the sheet.

Robert Bringhurst, in The Elements of Typographic Style, calls a line of about sixty-six characters ideal for a single column. This column is set a little wider than that.

None of this is decoration. The scribes who ruled their parchment before writing on it, and the printers who followed them, worked from canons of proportion in which the text block and its margins were derived together, from the shape of the page itself. In those schemes the outer margin is wider than the inner one and the foot is deeper than the head, so that two facing pages read as a single composed spread and the text appears to sit slightly high, as a picture does in a well-cut mount.

02What readers left there

The margin has a second life, which belongs to the reader. For as long as there have been books, people have written in them: corrections, cross-references, objections, small drawings, pointing hands. Whole traditions of scholarship were carried in the margins of other books, where commentary surrounded a text and was, in time, surrounded by commentary of its own.

The best-known note of this kind is also the most exasperating. Around 1637 Pierre de Fermat wrote in his copy of Diophantus’s Arithmetica that he had found a truly marvellous proof of a claim about powers of whole numbers, and that the margin was too small to contain it. The claim then stood unproved for more than three and a half centuries.

The proof came from Andrew Wiles, announced in 1993 and published, after a repair, in 1995. It runs to well over a hundred pages, so the margin was never going to be enough.

It is a good story about mathematics, and a better one about margins. The note survives only because the page had room for it. A book printed to its edges would have given Fermat nowhere to boast, and the most famous problem in number theory would have had to find another way into the world.

A margin is the part of the page that admits the reader.

That is the generous way to think about white space. The text is what the writer has to say. The margin is the writer’s admission that somebody else is present, who may have something to add.

03The case for leaving room

The idea travels well beyond paper. An engineer calls it tolerance, a builder calls it clearance, and anyone who keeps a calendar eventually learns to call it buffer. In each case the lesson is the one the page teaches. A thing designed to its exact limits works only for as long as nothing unexpected happens to it, which is never very long.

Leaving room can look like waste. The margin is paper that was paid for and not printed on; the empty hour is time that could have been sold. But the unused portion is doing a kind of work that is hard to see until it is gone. It absorbs error. It makes revision possible. It is where the second thought goes, and second thoughts are usually the better ones.

There is a discipline in this, and it is harder than it sounds, because the pressure always runs the other way. There is always one more paragraph that could be fitted in, one more feature, one more commitment. Filling the space is easy and feels productive. Defending the space requires believing that what is left out is contributing something.

04A note on this page

This page is a proof, in the printer’s sense: a first impression pulled to see how the type sits before anything is published. The words are placeholders. They are here so that the measure, the spacing, the headings and the notes can be judged with something real to read, since no one can tell whether a page is comfortable from a paragraph of scrambled Latin.

Nothing here is Sam Deddeh’s writing or opinion. The facts above are real; the essay exists only to be looked at.

So the design follows its own subject. The column is narrower than the screen allows. The notes stand in the margin on a wide screen and fold into the text on a narrow one. The rest is left empty, on purpose, and the hope is that it does what a margin has always done: make the words easier to stay with, and leave a little room for whoever is reading them.
